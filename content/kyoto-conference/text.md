Solar Regenerated Daily Cycle Passive Dehumidifying Air-Conditioner: Storing Drying Potential in Solid Desiccant Through Daily Weather Cycles

Adam Bin M Raman1, Hikaru Kobaysahi2
1 Graduate Student, Department of Architecture and Building Science, Tohoku University 2Professor, Department of Architecture and Building Science, Tohoku University

Abstract Air conditioning is a major energy consumer, particularly in humid regions due to the high latent heat factor.
(Boukhanouf et al., 2015) Studies in tropical climates indicate that air-conditioning is commonly used in bedrooms during sleeping hours when ambient humidity is elevated. (Kubota et al., 2010)

This paper examines the feasibility of a solar-driven desiccant dehumidification air-conditioning system that undergoes daily adsorption and desorption cycles to provide dehumidification for occupant comfort. The system utilizes free solar energy and a fan to force air through a delayed passive dehumidification process. During daytime hours, solar energy regenerates a charcoal desiccant material, storing its drying potential for later use. At night, ambient air is passed through the dry desiccant chamber, dehumidifying the air.

An experiment conducted in Sendai, Japan demonstrated this principle under real-world weather conditions. The results show a successful 40% reduction in relative humidity over a 12-hour period at night during high humidity weather, showcasing the potential for solar-powered dehumidification to supplement air conditioning.

Optimizing the system's performance involves considerations such as ventilation rate, solar energy capture efficiency, desiccant mass-to-surface area ratio, and the implementation of weather sensors to better preserve drying potential during rainy periods.

Keywords: Passive Techniques, Air Conditioning, Desiccant Humidity Control, Solar Regeneration, Daily Cycle

1. Introduction The International Energy Agency forecasts a
significant expansion in the global stock of air conditioners in buildings, from 1.6 billion currently to 5.6 billion by 2050, driven by factors such as population growth, rising incomes, falling equipment prices, and urbanization. This growth is particularly pronounced in the developing world, where usage levels currently lag behind more developed nations. (Bryant, 2019)
Studies conducted in Southeast Asia reveal that homeowners with air conditioning units typically install them in bedrooms and operate them predominantly at night during sleeping hours. (Kubota et al., 2010) These regions face a substantial cooling load due to the high latent heat in the ambient air at night. Conventional air conditioning systems typically cool the air below the dew point to remove moisture, which is an energy-intensive process.(Boukhanouf et al., 2015)

Observations indicate that the outdoor ambient temperature in a tropical city can reach as high as 36�C with 40% relative humidity during the day, while nighttime conditions can drop to as low as 24�C with 90% relative humidity. (Kubota, Chyee and Ahmad, 2009)
This suggests that the primary task for residential air conditioners in these regions is to dehumidify the air at night to achieve a comfort level of approximately 25�C and relative humidity below 65%. (ASHRAE, 2021)

1.2. Research Question This study investigates the feasibility of a passive or
semi-passive dehumidification approach that leverages the natural diurnal fluctuations in humidity. Specifically, it hypothesizes that the dry daytime air can be utilized to regenerate desiccant materials, which can then be employed for dehumidification during the humid nighttime hours.
Accordingly, this research aims to address the following research questions:
a. What level of passive air dehumidification can be achieved by leveraging the diurnal variations in humidity?
b. How effectively can desiccant materials absorb nocturnal humidity and then be regenerated using solar energy as the heat source?
c. To what extent can a desiccant material store dehumidification potential for later use when ambient humidity levels rise?
d. What are the climatic factors that need to be considered to potentially automate a passive humidity control system based on the natural humidity cycles?
2. Methods 2.1. Experiment System Design
The proposed system utilizes a solar-powered desiccant dehumidification approach, which features a substantial delay between the regeneration and dehumidification phases. The design aims to remove nighttime latent heat by passing outdoor air through a solid desiccant bed. The desiccant is then regenerated in the daytime, enabling repeated cycles of dehumidification and regeneration.
The physical experiment was conducted in Sendai, Japan, from July 2023 to June 2024 to assess the system's feasibility under real-world weather conditions. The location was selected to facilitate close monitoring of the experimental setup and apparatus. Recorded Data indicates that the monthly mean daily maximum temperature in Sendai during July and August 2022 was 28.7�C and 29.4�C, respectively, with monthly mean relative humidity of 85% and 81% respectively (`Japan Meteorological Agency', 2023). It was believed that the hot and humid summer conditions in Sendai, Japan could provide a reasonable comparison for the weather in other hot and humid regions relevant to the study.
Multiple experimental cycles were carried out, with the amount of desiccant and ventilation speed rates as the manipulated variables between cycles. The experiment was later repeated during the winter months

to evaluate the system's performance under colder conditions, as an additional finding for its potential applications.
2.2. Experiment Components The experimental apparatus is constructed within a
wooden frame box measuring 1m wide, 1m deep, and 1.5m tall as seen in
Fig. 1. The enclosure is fabricated from 6mm plywood panels with 50mm thick extruded polystyrene insulation lining the interior to minimize heat loss from the system. A skylight comprised of two 3mm thick twinwall polycarbonate panels with a 25mm air gap in between is installed at a 30-degree angle to the horizontal plane, facing the south. Underneath the skylight, a black perforated plywood panel serves to capture solar radiation.
Outdoor ambient air is drawn into the space between the skylight window and the perforated black plywood panel using a ventilation fan where it is heated by solar irradiance in the daytime. The plywood panel is perforated to allow air to pass through to the dehumidification chamber below.
Within the dehumidification chamber, air comes into contact with the desiccant material. Desiccants are placed in air-permeable bags atop a metal mesh. Here the desiccants interact with the air flow before being exhausted out from below.
Air is exhausted from the experimental enclosure by a timer-controlled ventilation fan equipped with oneway dampers. The fan operates from 8 AM to 4 PM during the solar irradiance period for desiccant regeneration, and from 6 PM to 6 AM during the nighttime period of increased relative humidity for the air dehumidification process.
2.3. Desiccant used Charcoal flakes derived from recycled wood was
utilized as solid desiccant owing to it being a sustainable and readily available material. The desiccants were distributed in air-permeable pouches into 1.5kg bags as seen in Fig. 15. Multiple bags were arranged on a metal mesh tray for constant weight measurement. Various quantities of bags were assessed throughout the experimental trials, ranging from 2 bags to the maximum of 6 bags.

4. Analysis 4.1. Ventilation Rate
The experiment manipulated ventilation rates as a controlled variable. The effects of various ventilation rates were averaged across multiple days in Fig. 10.
During the day, ventilation rates of 42 m�/hr and 48 m�/hr facilitated better regeneration compared to 61 m�/hr. The slower rates allowed more heat buildup, which enabled greater moisture release from the desiccant. However, further reducing the ventilation rate deteriorated the system's performance. The system could not expel enough moisture from within the enclosure at very low ventilation rates.
In contrast, slower ventilation rates down to 21 m�/hr performed better for nighttime dehumidification. Yet, the slowest rate of 6 m�/hr did not perform as well. The extra contact time between the air and the desiccant allowed for improved adsorption.
The optimal ventilation rates for the regeneration period differed from those that worked best during the dehumidification period.

4.3. Drying potential It has been observed that improved desiccant drying
during the day enhances the material's capacity to absorb moisture at night. By utilizing a lower ventilation rate, the additional solar radiation per unit air volume generates higher temperature air. Consequently, the desiccant releases more humidity, resulting in greater drying potential that enhances the adsorption performance in the dehumidification phase.
Fig. 12 compares two different ventilation rate settings during the regeneration period. The first setting used a ventilation rate of 35 m�/hr, leading to less desorption, while the second setting used a ventilation rate of 26 m�/hr, resulting in better desorption. The nighttime ventilation rate was the same for both at 26 m�/hr. The desiccant that underwent better regeneration exhibited a higher drying potential in the dehumidification phase.

5.2. Weather Awareness The timer-controlled ventilation system used in the
experiment attempts to regenerate the desiccant material even on rainy days, which conversely leads to an increase in humidity within the system instead of regeneration as seen on 6 and 8 September in Fig. 14. This ultimately diminishes the system's dehumidification capacity and performance when it is needed.
An enhanced smart sensor-based system could be programmed to monitor the external weather conditions and intelligently deactivate the ventilation on high humidity days, thereby preserving the drying potential of the desiccant material within.

5.3. Thermal Barrier Solid desiccants have the potential to serve as
thermal buffers for buildings. The solar energy absorbed by the desiccant is initially utilized to change liquid water into water vapor, which may help mitigate sensible heat gain into the occupied spaces. As demonstrated in Fig. 4, the air temperature experiences a significant decrease upon regenerating the desiccant. This hints at its use as a regenerative heat shield as a temperature Phase change material.
5.4. Winter Potential Evaluations of the system during the winter months
yielded comparable outcomes. This suggests that the system can be leveraged to extract moisture from the nighttime atmosphere and to subsequently release it under solar irradiance. This process can be harnessed to provide passive humidification through the drier winter months.
Even though the absolute humidity levels are lower during the winter season compared to summer, the system's performance remains substantial when assessed in terms of relative humidity.
5.5. Computer simulation Developing the system and refining the component
designs would benefit from a computer simulation approach. The existing physical experimental method takes multiple days for data collection and is vulnerable to volatile weather conditions. This makes it almost impossible to replicate multiple specific scenarios for direct data comparison. A simulated environment would enable the assessment of diverse factors influencing performance, leading to consistent and reliable outcomes.
It is necessary to evaluate different scenarios to comprehend and refine the system's performance. This involves exploring various system designs, simulating the long-term energy cost advantages, and assessing the systems' implementation in diverse geographical locations across the globe.
5.6. Climatic variations The author notes that the system is conceptualized
based on the air conditioning usage patterns of hot and humid regions. Although this research was conducted in Sendai, the results obtained here show that it may be applicable to tropical regions as well.
The system configuration may necessitate some finetuning in different geographic areas. This is because the system's operation is contingent upon solar radiation, weather conditions, and the air conditioning needs of each location. For instance, hot-humid or cold-humid regions may require sufficient drying capacity, whereas dry regions can utilize a desiccant that spreads the releases moisture throughout the day.
Further research is needed to investigate the system's performance in diverse climatic conditions to enhance its real-world applicability.

6. Conclusions The system presents promising prospects for
reducing air-conditioning demands during both summer and winter by harnessing passive renewable energies that tap into the daily humidity fluctuations in the surrounding air. Presently, the research remains at an early stage, necessitating further refinement to fully unlock the potential of the phenomena and to utilize it for passive comfort air conditioning.
The passive processes and natural energy source of the system render it highly dependent on the Earth's natural rhythms, suggesting that a method to synchronize the system design with the Earth's rhythm could enhance the system performance.
Moving forward, constructing a full-scale prototype for household cooling tests would be advantageous to gather application-specific data.
